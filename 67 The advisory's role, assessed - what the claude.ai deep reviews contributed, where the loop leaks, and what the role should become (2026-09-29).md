# The advisory's role, assessed: what the claude.ai deep reviews contributed, where the loop leaks, and what the role should become (2026-09-29)

Companion to `_advisory/README.md` (the convention and the assimilation log), the assimilation memos 32 to 49 and their verification ledgers (34b to 49b), `64 Regional sweep design ... (2026-09-28).md`, `66 The novice column of file 14 ... (2026-09-29).md`, and `67a Asks register, seeded with the advisory's open asks on seven live NC-VA rows (2026-09-29).csv`.

## 0. Answer first

The advisory should continue, but not in the job it has been doing. As a second reader of evidence and as the campaign's best researcher of statutes, ordinances and municipal records it has earned its place: of the 395 claims the verification passes tested, 79 percent reproduced against primary sources, and its findings closed rows that would otherwise have cost a due-diligence fee (a phantom $20,000 sale on Pecan Dr, a 2017 photo set on Tamarack Dr, a plat that makes Conover Rd one house, a $500,000 blanket lien on Edgar St). As the campaign's engine it has not worked, for four reasons that are the campaign's more than the advisory's:

1. **The loop has no outbound leg.** The advisory has written 323 asks (leases, rent rolls, determination letters, permits, bills) across thirty row-update files. File 14 and the memos record no call, letter, e-mail or reply on any of them. The cheapest kills on every live row are those asks, and desk research cannot close them. Memo 29 set "Track B: offers on the verified names, now; costs nothing but conversations" on 9/14; fifteen days later there has been no offer and no conversation.
2. **Effort ran inversely to probability.** The six rows the board still rates at 0.2 or better (Day St, Choate, Lafayette, Carver, Lee St, 1708 Birchwood) were last reviewed on 9/15 to 9/16. The twenty deep reviews of 9/23 to 9/25 all ended at P 0.12 or lower, and about two thirds of them ended on a rent or price failure that the listing's own rent statement or a ten-minute comp pull would have shown before the records were pulled.
3. **The record grew faster than the decision.** Two boards (the decision boards 32a to 49a, now 977 columns wide, and file 14) hold different versions of the same rows; the novice column lag fixed in memo 66 was one symptom; six Virginia deep reviews delivered on 9/25 (Spruce, Rosemont, Elm, Mariner, High St, Industrial) have not been assimilated; the local stream wrote 7.3 MB of assimilation for 2.1 MB of advisory, eight rounds of it in one night.
4. **The campaign moved and the advisory did not.** Memos 64 and 65 do not mention it. Seventy-two of the 136 rows on the board are now in Pennsylvania, Ohio, West Virginia, Maryland and New York at a median price of about $90,000 (48 of them under $100,000), where the advisory's North Carolina and Virginia record recipes do not apply and where file 08's ten percent bid cap is $2,500 to $10,000 a building.

What the role should become is in section 6: a blind second reader on the few rows that reach the offer stage, the author of one reusable playbook per regional market, the drafter of the asks that a register now tracks to a sent date and a reply, and the writer of a one-page decision brief that the deep review supports rather than replaces. Section 7 lists frameworks and perspectives not yet deployed that would make each property's record more accurate and more complete: kill-shot ordering by value of information, a claims ledger in long form, calibration against HUD property condition reports, a seller-motivation index from records, market-as-inspector priors, an inter-rater statistic on the photo grades, and a file 08b for sub-$100,000 stock.

Its output falls into four kinds.

The photo-provenance hashing that appeared on 9/23 (Tamarack's five frames hash-matched to an October 2017 MLS set) is the advisory's own method and one of its best.

## 2. What the verification passes made of it: the scorecard

Rounds 4 to 19 (memos 34 to 49) each carry a ledger of the advisory's claims against county, city, Register of Deeds, ordinance, HUD, FEMA, DEQ and listing sources. Aggregated:

| | Rows | Share of advisory claims |
|---|---|---|

| Reproduced (confirmed, reproduced, extended, sharpened, carried) | 313 | 79% |
| Corrected or revised | 64 | 16% |
| Refuted | 6 | 1.5% |
| Open, not re-verified or not readable | 12 | 3% |
| New items the local pass added on its own | 87 | 18% of all ledger rows |

The 79 percent is the case for the advisory: its record pulls are mostly right, its zoning readings are careful, and its GIS endpoints were adopted as cited from the first week. The 16 percent has a shape worth naming, because it says what a second pass must always do:

- **Vintage errors, the largest class.** The North Carolina dwelling-insurance step applied twice (three advisories in a row put $1,764 on 10/1/2026 when the board's $1,680 already carried the step); FY2026 fair market rents where FY2027 had published; 2025 tax bills where 2026 bills existed (Oxford's three-cent rate rise, Cumberland's, Durham's); revaluation years (2033 for 2031, 2029 for 2028, "not published" for 2029); a stale zoning frame (Fayetteville's Ordinance S2025-008 of 4/14/2025 made two- to four-family dwellings permitted in SF-6, so the advisory's "lawful nonconforming use with a 365-day clock" was a year out of date).
- **Field-semantics errors.** The Cumberland assessor's CONDITION field read as a letter grade (A versus F) when it is a condition class plus a depreciation percentage; Redfin's assessment column read as the tax base; a Wilson "Federally Recognized" historic category read as design review.
- **Assumptions stated as facts.** "Unencumbered" on Edgar St (the Register of Deeds carried a $500,000 New Republic Bank deed of trust covering three properties); "no code cases" on Star St (48 cases since 2005); "not a liquidation pattern" on Auburndale's seller (two sales under list in 2026); "fresh stump" on Jacob St (in the county's 2023 photo); a Pecan Dr sale that had no instrument.
- **Deed-chain misreads.** Excise stamps on the wrong instrument (2108 Rogers), a quitclaim read as a sale, a $59,800 note that the image says is $50,800.

The 87 new items are the case for the verification pass. Several were decisive: the Edgar lien, the Star code history, the Pecan deed chain, Auburndale's documented rent roll (which turned a pass into a fail), Church St's creek boundary, Scarborough's density nonconformity, the Fayetteville fee stack applied to eight rows. The pass is not a formality.

The cost side: 18 assimilation memos in twelve days (one each on 9/15, 9/16, 9/19 and 9/20, two on 9/23, two on 9/24, eight on 9/25, two on 9/26), 7.3 MB of memos, boards, ledgers and record sets against 2.1 MB of advisory input, and a queue of six reviews that stopped when the regional expansion began on 9/27. The two streams write about 100 to 150 KB of text per property for rows whose final probability is under one in ten.

## 3. What it changed on the board

Every deep review moved a row, almost always down. The assimilated verdicts: Kemper ENTRANT at P 0.12; Gilmer Cir ENTRANT-HOLD at 0.08; Choate promoted (9/16); Granville HOLD 0.09; Edgar NEAR-MISS 0.05; Hugh Caldwell 0.05; Rogers 0.03; Daughtry 0.03; Auburndale 0.03 conditional; Varsity 0.02; Pinetops 0.02; Ranch Farm 0.02; Jacob 0.015; Scarborough 0.01; Star 0.01; Tamarack 0.01; Pecan 0.005; Church 0.005; Conover 0; Eden parked in contract; Troy NEAR-MISS with a financing hold; Nickey NEAR-MISS; Squirrel St 0.04 each; Washington St HOLD for documents.

Summing the latest probability on each of the 46 rows of decision board 49a gives 3.7 expected closings, of which 1.7 sits in six rows (Day St 0.33, Choate 0.33, Lafayette 0.33, Carver 0.30, Lee St 0.25, 1708 Birchwood 0.20). Those six probabilities date from 9/15 and 9/16. Ten rows sit under 0.02, thirteen between 0.02 and 0.05, seventeen between 0.05 and 0.2. The pattern is plain: the campaign's heaviest analytical instrument was pointed at its thinnest prospects, and its best prospects have had nothing since the photo pass of the first week. Lafayette, the highest-scored NC-VA row, still carried a GREEN-verify condition cell until memo 66 corrected it to the YELLOW the 9/15 read had found.

None of this makes the reviews wasted. Loss avoidance is real money for a buyer with $166,451 who loses a due-diligence fee on every walk. But the campaign has bought nothing, offered on nothing and asked nothing, and the record it has built is a record of why not to buy twenty-eight buildings.

## 4. Logical gaps and blind spots

**4.1 No outbound leg.** Every deep review ends in a ranked list of asks to the city, the agent, the seller, the lender, the utility. Advisory 62's first action is "one written request to City of Danville Planning" with the account number, the map number and three questions; its second is a message to the brokerage listing seven documents. Nothing in the record shows a request sent or answered, on any row, since 9/14. The probability chains keep a "seller acceptance" factor and a "determination letter" factor as guesses because the one instrument that would resolve them is never requested. The asks are also where the cheapest kills live: a rent roll answers Gate 6 in a day; a determination letter answers Gate 3; both cost a phone call.

**4.2 An unstated selection rule.** Which row gets a deep review appears to follow which photos were placed in `_evidence`, not the board's probabilities. The six rows the board rates highest have no deep review after 9/16; twenty rows rated under 0.12 got one between 9/23 and 9/25. No memo states a rule.

**4.3 The cheapest kill comes last.** In roughly twenty of the twenty-eight reviews the row failed Gate 5 or 6 at the ask on the listing's own rents or on comps (Granville, Church, Auburndale, Daughtry, Star, Jacob, Pecan, Tamarack, Rogers, Edgar, Varsity, Nickey, Hugh Caldwell, Troy, Eden, Scarborough, the Squirrel four, Washington St, Industrial, Rosemont). The record pull, the Street View history, the DEQ query and the deed chain were done anyway. Each review has a section ordering (photos, record, zoning, Street View, environment, market, underwriting, unknowns, probability); the underwriting is section 7 of 13.

**4.4 Two boards, one truth.** The advisory's row updates target decision-board ids (`45a id 16`); the routines write to file 14; the decision boards carry every dated column ever added (`novice_0915` ... `novice_0925n`, 977 columns on 49a) and the operative value has to be found by eye. Memo 66 found seven file 14 cells that had lagged the verified reads for up to fourteen days. The last two row updates (56c, 57c) already target file 14 directly, which is the right direction.

**4.5 Anchoring, and the limits of independence.** The advisory reads the board and the memos before it reviews, so its verdicts are readings of the same frame: P moves 0.12 to 0.08 or 0.02 to 0.01, rarely against the local view. Its refutation rate in the ledgers (1.5 percent) is low partly because it is careful and partly because it starts where the local session ended. The one class of finding that needs independence most, the photo read, is the one where the advisory sees the local grade first. The two streams also share a model family and the same public web, so the independence is procedural, not epistemic; that is a reason to buy ground truth (section 7.3), not a reason to drop the second reader.

**4.6 Vintage drift is the characteristic error.** Most corrections are dates and rates. The pipeline now carries dated constants (`constants.json`, the FY2027 FMR columns, the insurance ledger with its 10/1/2026 step); the advisory re-derives them from the web each session and lands a year stale. It should consume the campaign's dated constants and challenge them, not recompute them.

**4.7 No calibration, anywhere.** Forty-six probabilities and twenty-eight condition grades rest on no ground truth: no inspection has been ordered, no unit walked, no deal closed, no appraisal read. The probability chains multiply four to six subjective factors as if independent (advisory 62: 0.33 × 0.45 × 0.30 × 0.60 = 0.027) and are then "displayed with a 10x allowance" when the product looks too small. Reported to three decimals, they are guesses with a formula. The C-ratings are read from marketing photographs whose provenance the campaign only began checking on 9/23.

**4.8 Analyst-facing, not decision-facing.** A review runs 25 to 39 KB; its asks are in section 0 and again in section 12; the buyer (Ogo) has no one-page brief that says what the building is, what it is worth to the plan, the three things that would kill it, the price, and the questions to send today. The decision that matters most, whether to make the phone call, is buried under the record.

**4.9 The regional expansion has no advisory.** Memos 64 and 65 designed and appraised the four routines without a role for it. The 72 regional rows sit at $24,900 to $210,000, 48 of them under $100,000, in counties whose records live in different systems (Pennsylvania's base-year assessments and common-level ratios, Ohio's sexennial reappraisals and county auditor sites, New York's RPS rolls, West Virginia's state parcel map, Maryland's SDAT) and under different landlord law (New York's Good Cause Eviction, Philadelphia's rental license and certificate of rental suitability, Maryland's right of first refusal, Johnstown's sewer lateral certification). None of that is in a playbook. File 08's ten percent bid cap becomes $2,500 on a $25,000 Johnstown duplex, so every regional YELLOW walks under the rule as written; nobody owns that problem.

**4.10 Photo evidence is human-ferried and read once.** Najum downloads each MLS gallery by hand; the pipeline fetches one lead photo per shortlisted row; galleries, county orthophotos, oblique imagery and Street View capture dates are pulled ad hoc when a reviewer thinks of them (Daughtry's 2008 to 2024 Street View captures, Church St's 2013 HUD set). Provenance hashing caught the Tamarack and Church St reuse, but it is not a step every review runs.

**4.11 The suggestion channel is unaudited.** The advisory has made 306 pipeline and campaign suggestions. Adoption is recorded in prose ("the advisory's seven suggestions are accepted", "six accepted with two amendments") and many were adopted (the rate triple, the fee decomposition, the rent-ask parse, the meter-count rule). There is no register with a status per item, so nobody can say how many are open.

**4.12 Latency against listing survival.** Memo 63 showed that rows above 1.8 percent rent-to-price rarely survive to the sweep (0.5 of 33); the good stock moves in days. A deep review takes a day to write and one to two to assimilate. Four rows changed state during or after their review (Eden pending the morning its review arrived, Star withdrawn two days before its assimilation, Conover's A side sold five months before its review, Rosemont contingent three days after). Depth that arrives after the listing is gone is a record, not a decision.

## 5. Should the role continue?

Yes, on three conditions, and with a different job description.

**Keep, because it works:** the statute and ordinance reading (Va. Code 15.2-2307(F) on Industrial Ave; Fayetteville's 30-7.C routing; Clayton's casualty clause; the HUD unvented-heater rule; the RRP thresholds once corrected), the record-pull recipes it leaves in its b-files (transport maps of which endpoint answered), the provenance hashing, the asks lists, the unknown-unknowns sections, and the sheer second pair of eyes on photographs. Its errors are the kind a checklist and dated constants remove.

**Stop, because it does not pay:** exhaustive record pulls on rows that fail Gate 5 or 6 at the ask; reviews of rows without a fresh status check; parallel sessions that collide on numbering; reviews written to the decision boards rather than to file 14; recomputing constants the pipeline carries.

**Change, because the campaign changed:** the unit of work moves from "one property, everything about it" to two products that scale across 72 regional rows and a weekly fold of fifty per region: a market playbook per metro, written once and reused, and a decision brief per offer-stage row. The per-property deep review survives only for rows that pass the cheapest kills first.

The three conditions: (1) an asks register with sent dates, so the loop closes (67a seeds it); (2) a selection rule tied to the board's probability and status, so the reviews go where the money is; (3) a brief for the advisory that tells it what changed on 9/28 (memo 64 section 2, the tiers, the regions, the jurisdiction flags, the novice grammar of memo 66), because it currently does not know.

## 6. The redesigned loop

**6.1 The queue.** A row enters the review queue only when it is ACTIVE on today's refresh, passes Gate 5 on its own stated rent or on a comp pull, has a unit count evidenced by something other than the remarks (E911 points, meters, water accounts, the county card), and sits in tier A or B or has a rent-to-price above the tier C bar. That is a ten-minute screen the local session can run from the sweep's own facts. Rows that pass get the asks sent (6.2) before any records are pulled.

**6.2 The asks register (67a).** One row per ask: property, the ask, the addressee, who drafted it, when it was sent, by what channel, when it was answered, the outcome, and whether the answer kills or clears the row. The advisory drafts (it already writes the letters); Najum or an agent sends; the local session logs the reply into the record. The weekly count of asks sent is the campaign's most honest progress metric, because it is the only one that touches the world. 67a seeds it with the 48 asks the advisory has already written for Day St, Choate, Lafayette, Carver, Lee St, Pinetops and Gilmer Cir; the first ten to send are the leases and rent rolls, because they answer Gate 6.

**6.3 Blind second read, then the deep review.** For a row that clears 6.1 and whose asks are out, the advisory receives the evidence folder, the sweep's `facts.json` and the photos, and nothing else: no verdict, no grade, no memo. It grades the photos and states its own gates. The local session compares; disagreements are the findings and go to the top of the brief. Only then, if the row is still alive, does the full records pull run, and it runs against the playbook (6.4) so that the property-specific part is hours, not days.

**6.4 Market playbooks.** One file per metro on the board (Johnstown, McKees Rocks and Pittsburgh, Charleroi, New Castle, Parkersburg, Huntington, Cumberland MD, Cleveland, Toledo, Akron, Canton, Dayton, Martins Ferry, Binghamton, Johnson City, Elmira, Corning, Scranton, Philadelphia), each answering the same questions once: where the card, the deeds, the permits and the code cases are and which endpoints answer plain fetches; the assessment system and its trap (Pennsylvania's base-year values and the common-level ratio, and whether school districts appeal assessments after a sale; Ohio's reappraisal cycle and the effective-rate arithmetic; New York's equalization rates); rental registration, inspection and licensing; lead-paint and lead-line programs; sewer lateral rules at sale; landlord-tenant law and eviction timelines; who pays heat and water in the local convention and what winter heat costs a tenant; property-management fee floors and whether a manager will take a $40,000 building; insurance appetite for pre-1930 frame stock; the two-to-four-unit sales volume and the buyer pool at exit (memo 63's area workbook). The advisory is the right author: this is statute, ordinance and municipal record work, its strongest suit, and one playbook serves every row in the metro for the life of the campaign.

**6.5 The decision brief.** One page per offer-stage row, in plain language for the buyer: what the building is and who lives in it; the number at 6.75 and 7.40 on the documented roll; the price at which it clears the bar; the three things most likely to kill it and how each is settled; the asks sent and outstanding; the offer terms and contingencies. The deep review is the appendix.

**6.6 One ledger.** Replace dated columns with a long-form claims ledger: property, attribute, value, source, instrument, date, status (claimed, reproduced, corrected, refuted, superseded), superseded by. File 14 becomes a view of the latest status per attribute; the advisory's c-files map onto it one row per claim; the verification pass becomes a status change rather than a new column; the novice cell, the probability and the verdict are each the latest row for that attribute. This is what 43c and 49c already are in spirit ("record sets" with hundreds of fields); the change is the shape, not the content.

**6.7 Cadence.** A review is commissioned on a row, not a session; it returns within 24 hours or the row is re-queued; the local session assimilates within 24 hours or writes "not yet assimilated" into the row (memo 66 did this for Rosemont and Mariner); the six Virginia reviews of 9/25 are either assimilated this week or marked so.

## 7. Frameworks, connections and perspectives not yet deployed

**7.1 Kill-shot ordering by value of information.** For each open question on a row, estimate the probability the answer kills the row, the cost of getting the answer (minutes, dollars, days), and the cost the campaign avoids by killing early (the hours of records work, the due-diligence fee). Order the questions by avoided cost times kill probability divided by cost to answer. On the campaign's own numbers the order is nearly always: today's status; price against the 8 percent price on the stated rent; unit count evidence; the zoning use table; the rent roll ask; the photo read; then everything else. The deep reviews run this order backwards.

**7.2 The market as inspector.** Seven of the eleven most-reviewed rows had a contract fall through in 2026; the reviews explained why (asbestos siding, a $20,000 basis, a stripped HVAC, a 2017 photo set). Days on market, price cuts, relists and fall-throughs are inspection results the market has already paid for. Use them as a prior: a fresh listing or a back-on-market row gets the offer attention; a 389-day listing with six cuts gets the walk-away presumption and a short review that looks only for the reason. The pipeline already parses history events; a `market_inspection` score (days on market, cuts, fall-throughs, relists, portfolio transfers) would set the review depth.

**7.3 Buy ground truth.** Three cheap sources of calibration exist and none has been used. (a) HUD Home Store listings carry a Property Condition Report written by an inspector; Cleveland, Toledo, Dayton, Akron, Pittsburgh and Binghamton have such listings in the campaign's price band. Grade their photos blind with the file 08 rule, then open the report: the confusion matrix between photo grade and inspector finding is the campaign's first measured error rate. (b) One paid walk-through video by a local agent or a showing service on the highest-probability live row, compared with the desk grade. (c) When an offer is finally made, the inspection report against the review that preceded it. Track a Brier score on the probability chains as verdicts resolve (sold to someone else, withdrawn, closed by Ogo). Until then, report probabilities to one significant figure and drop the "10x allowance".

**7.4 A seller-motivation index from records.** The advisory found, one property at a time, delinquent taxes paid by a foreclosure attorney, estate deeds, LLC dissolutions, portfolio transfers, a lender-flagged bill, a seller with a litigation record. Score them: each is a public-record fact with a known source, and together they estimate the "seller accepts a price under the ask" factor that the chains currently guess at 0.05 to 0.35. Calibrate the score against the county duplex sale cohorts the advisory already pulls (Gilmer's, Church St's): list-to-sale ratios by motivation class are the base rate the offer strategy needs.

**7.5 Inter-rater reliability on the photo grades.** With two graders (the weekly judgment in the sweep and the advisory's blind read), track agreement on the C-rating and on RED items as a running statistic. Agreement is not accuracy, but disagreement is exactly the set of rows where a third look or a walk-through buys the most, and the drift in agreement over weeks shows whether the graders are converging on a shared rule or on each other.

**7.6 File 08b for sub-$100,000 stock.** The rule's ten percent bid cap was written for $175,000 to $250,000 buildings. For a $40,000 duplex in Johnstown or Cleveland, replace the percentage with an absolute make-ready budget tied to the cash left after closing (file 08's own arithmetic gives $87,951 at a $200,000 price and far more at $40,000) and a rent-recovery test (months of net rent to recover the make-ready). Add the cold-climate RED list the current list lacks: steam or hot-water boilers past life, knob-and-tube in balloon-framed stock, lead service lines, sewer laterals that fail the sale certification, slate and tar roofs, a gas service that was cut and never restored. The advisory is the natural author.

**7.7 Automate the advisory's mechanical steps.** The sweep can fetch the whole gallery for every ENTRANT row and hash it against the seen index's earlier sets (the Tamarack check as a pipeline step); pull the county card, the E911 points and the meter count where an API answers (the b-files record which ones do); capture the Street View dates. What is left for the advisory is judgment, which is what it is for.

**7.8 Perspectives the record lacks.** The lender and the appraiser: two-to-four-unit comparables are scarce in small markets, so an appraisal gap is a live risk on any offer near the 8 percent price; one conversation with an appraiser per metro would set the discount. The insurer: memo 29 asked for two real quotes to calibrate the $1,500, $1,600 and $3,000 constants; none exists, and pre-1930 frame stock with a stripped HVAC or a boarded opening may have no appetite at all. The property manager: memo 30 said management is not a flat ten percent; in the regional markets a $100 to $150 monthly minimum per unit on a $650 rent is 15 to 23 percent, and some managers will not take the building. The tenant: who pays heat, and what a winter costs on tenant-paid electric baseboard in Binghamton, sets the achievable rent more than the SAFMR does. The exit buyer: memo 63's volumes say who buys a Johnstown duplex in 2031, which is what the price should be tested against. The buyer herself: the campaign has never written down what Ogo will and will not do at 400 miles, and every tier-C row assumes an answer.

**7.9 A pre-mortem.** If in March 2027 no duplex has been bought, the record already says why: the loop produced records, not offers. Run the campaign backwards from an offer: what has to be true by Friday for a written offer to go out on the best live row, and which of those things is a phone call.
